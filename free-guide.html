AI in Mental-Health Practice: What the Evidence Actually Shows

A short, rigorously-sourced briefing for counselors, social workers, psychologists, and allied practitioners — cutting through the hype to what the research supports today.

AI tools are entering mental-health practice faster than the evidence — or the ethics guidance — can keep up. This guide summarizes what the peer-reviewed research actually supports, what it doesn't, and the safeguards every practitioner should have in place before using an AI tool with clients.

1. The evidence is early — and mostly uncontrolled

Most consumer-facing mental-health AI has been evaluated in small, preliminary, non-randomized studies. These can be encouraging, but they are not the controlled trials clinicians rely on for treatment decisions.

What one widely-cited study found. A real-world evaluation of the conversational agent Wysa reported symptom improvement on the PHQ-9 among higher-engagement users (mean 5.84 vs. 3.52). Read carefully: it was a preliminary, uncontrolled, non-randomized mixed-methods evaluation, and two of three authors were affiliated with the product.
Inkster, Sarda & Subramanian (2018). JMIR mHealth uHealth, 6(11):e12106. doi:10.2196/12106

Takeaway: treat AI tools as adjuncts to be monitored, not as validated interventions. Ask, for any tool: what is the actual study design, and who funded it?

2. Bias is a clinical safety issue, not just a technical one

AI systems learn from historical data, and historical data encodes inequity. In mental-health contexts, that can mean unequal accuracy across groups.

  • Facial-analysis systems showed error rates far higher for darker-skinned women than lighter-skinned men — the "Gender Shades" finding that put algorithmic bias on the map.
  • Bias in clinical decision-support can compound documented disparities, such as the systematic under-assessment of pain in Black patients.

Buolamwini & Gebru, Gender Shades · Hoffman KM et al. (2016), PNAS, doi:10.1073/pnas.1516047113

Takeaway: before adopting a tool, ask whether it was validated across the populations you serve — and watch for differential performance in your own use.

3. Privacy and consent duties already apply

You don't need new rules to use AI ethically — your existing obligations govern it. Putting client information into a consumer AI tool can implicate confidentiality and, in litigation, even privilege.

  • NASW Code §§1.03 and 1.07, plus the 2017 Technology Standards, govern technology-assisted services.
  • HIPAA and 42 CFR Part 2 (2024 revision) constrain what may be disclosed and to whom.
  • Informed consent for technology-assisted services is an explicit ethical requirement (e.g., ACA §H.2.a).

Takeaway: use tools with contractual confidentiality (or run them locally), and build AI-specific informed-consent language into your intake.

A 30-second checklist before you use any AI tool with clients:
  • What is the study design behind its claims — and who funded it?
  • Was it validated across the populations I serve?
  • Where does my input go, and can it be used to train the model or shared?
  • Have I obtained informed consent for technology-assisted services?
  • Am I keeping human clinical judgment in the loop?
