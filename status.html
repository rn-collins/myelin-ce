What “accredited” actually means, and why it matters here

Accreditation is not a property of a course. It is a status an accrediting body confers on a provider, against a published standard, for a fixed term — and that distinction is the whole reason this page exists.

Two concrete examples, because the abstraction is slippery. The ANSI/IACET 1-2018 Standard for Continuing Education and Training assesses how a provider builds its programme across nine categories — among them needs analysis, learning outcomes, assessment of those outcomes, and awarding the IACET CEU and maintaining learner records. In continuing medical education, the ACCME's Accreditation Criteria require providers to meet Core Criteria plus the Standards for Integrity and Independence, and grant accreditation for defined terms — two years provisionally, four years on full accreditation, six with commendation.

Read either standard and the same thing stands out: a large share of it is about the machinery around the teaching. Who verified the learner was who they claimed to be. Who holds the record, in what form, for how long. Who is independent of the commercial interests involved. Myelin has none of that machinery, deliberately — there is no account, no identity check, and no server-side record — and that is exactly why nothing here can carry credit. The material can be good and the credit can still be zero. Those are separate claims, and this site only makes the first one.

How to check whether something counts for you

If you need hours that a board or employer will accept, the ordering below is the one that avoids wasted money. It is generic guidance, not advice about your licence — your own board's rules govern.

  1. Start with your board, not with the course. Find which accrediting bodies your licensing board or employer recognises. This is the step people skip, and it is the only one that determines the answer.
  2. Check the provider's status, then the activity. Accreditation attaches to the provider; individual activities are then offered under it. A provider being accredited does not mean every item it publishes is an accredited activity.
  3. Ask what record you will receive and who holds it. If nobody but you holds the record, nobody can attest to it later. That is the practical difference between a certificate and a printout.
  4. Ask how identity was verified. An activity that cannot say who completed it cannot report who completed it.
  5. Be suspicious of the word “certificate” standing alone. It is unregulated in most contexts. “Certificate of completion” from an unaccredited provider and “accredited CE” are different objects with similar names.

What this site is for instead

Non-credit does not mean unserious; it means the value has to come from the material rather than from the paperwork. What Myelin can honestly offer is practice at decisions you will actually face: separating a claim from the evidence for it, deciding what may enter a tool before you paste it, and defining human oversight that is real rather than ceremonial. Every course ends in a record you can export and keep, and every brief ends in an artefact you could hand to someone else.

Two legitimate uses that do not require accreditation: preparing for a conversation with the person who does hold the risk, and building an evidence trail showing your team thought about this before something went wrong. Neither needs a credit hour. Both need the record.
